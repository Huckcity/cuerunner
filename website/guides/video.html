Video and outputs

Pictures go to outputs — windows Cuerunner keeps open for the show, one per projector or screen — and three cue types put them there: Video, Image and Text. All of it is free.

Adding an output

Open the Outputs tab in the dock and press + Add output. A black window opens: that is the output, and it stays open for as long as the show has it, so a cue that follows another lands on a picture that was never taken down — there is no black frame between cues. Each output's card shows a live picture of what it is showing, so you can see the projector from the desk.

Display puts it somewhere: A window you can drag and size, or one of this computer's displays, with Fullscreen to fill it. A fullscreen output never takes the keyboard, so GO stays with the cue list. Where an output goes is kept on this computer, not in the show file: the same show opens in rehearsal on a laptop and on the night on the venue's projectors, and each machine remembers its own places. The output's name, its size, its timer and its projector settings travel with the show.

A display that is not connected when the show opens is not a failure: the output opens as a window instead, with a red banner across it saying which display it was meant for, and its card says the same. It moves back as soon as the display is plugged in.

Identify draws each output's name across it for three seconds. Test pattern puts a grid, the centre, the corners and colour bars over one output, at its own resolution, for lining a projector up.

A video cue

Add one from the toolbar's More menu, then Browse on its Video tab. MP4 (H.264), WebM (VP8, VP9, AV1) and the other formats Chromium decodes all play. A small preview of the file sits under its name. Start time and End time trim it, Loop count repeats it (−1 loops it until it is stopped or a devamp cue lets it go), and Hold last frame keeps the final frame up when it ends — the cue then runs until it is stopped, the way a held picture does.

While it plays, the Video tab has Pause/Resume and a Position slider. A paused video stands on the frame it is on, its sound taken away, and resumes from exactly there.

Its sound

A video's sound plays through the audio engine as an ordinary voice — the same level, pan, routing matrix, fades and meters an audio cue has, on the Sound tab — and the picture is silent. Both start on the engine's clock at the same instant, and the output keeps the picture on that clock frame by frame: a small drift is closed by playing the picture a touch fast or slow, a large one by moving it. However long the video, picture and sound stay together. A fade cue on the video's Volume fades the sound; a stop cue with a fade takes both down together.

A video with no sound track plays as a picture alone, and its cue ends with it. The sound of an MP4 or MOV plays everywhere; a WebM's or MKV's needs a decoder this machine has — on Windows that is the system's own, and where it has none the picture plays silent and the Sound tab says so.

Images and text

An Image cue shows a still — PNG, JPEG, GIF, WebP, SVG and the rest — for Shown for seconds, or until it is stopped when that is empty. A Text cue draws its text in the font, size, colour and style you give it, anchored to one of nine places on the output.

Layers, fades and crossfades

Every picture cue has a Picture tab. Output says which output it goes to (empty is the show's first). Layer stacks it: higher is in front, and pictures on one layer stack in the order they were fired, the newest in front — so a title on layer 5 sits over every video on layer 1. Pictures layer; nothing is taken down because another went up, unless you ask for it.

Fade in starts on the picture's first frame on screen, not on the GO, so a file that takes a moment to open never jumps in half-faded. Fade out lands on the picture's own end — the end of a video's last pass, or of an image's or text's display time.

Crossfade dissolves a video or image in from whatever is on its output at or below its layer, over its fade-in. The dissolve is exact: half way through, the output is half the old frame and half the new, so nothing dips through black and a letterboxed video's bars dissolve to black rather than to the picture it replaced. When it lands, what it replaced is taken down and its cue is stopped; a video it replaced takes its sound down over the same time. With nothing on the output, a crossfade simply fades up. A title on a layer above stays exactly where it is.

A fade cue aimed at a picture can move its Opacity, with the same curves and Stop target when done a sound fade has. Opacity on the Picture tab is the level a picture rests at; changing it while the picture is up changes it at once.

Where a picture sits

Size to fits the picture inside the output (letterboxed), fills it (cropping what spills over), stretches it to the output's shape, or shows it native, one pixel per pixel. Then Position moves it as a share of the output, Scale sizes it, Rotate turns it clockwise, and Crop cuts from each edge first. Every one of them applies while the picture is up.

Corner pin and soft edges

A projector that is not square to its screen throws a keystoned picture. Corner pin and soft edges, on the output's card, moves each of the four corners by a share of the output, and the whole frame — text and the timer included — is laid onto that shape in perspective. Soft edge fades an edge to black across a share of the frame, for two projectors whose pictures overlap; the edge curve bends that ramp for the projectors' gamma. Both are part of the show, because they belong to the venue's rig as the show was teched on it.

An output draws on the computer's GPU. On a machine whose graphics Chromium will not use it draws the same picture with its 2D fallback, says so on its card, and puts a corner pin's offsets aside — that one thing needs the GPU — with a banner saying so.

Timers

Timer on an output's card draws a clock over it: the time of day, the show clock (from the first GO since the show was opened), or the time left on the most recently started cue that has an end. Floating timer, in the panel's header, opens a small window that stays on top of everything else on this computer, counting the same way — for the operator, not the audience.

When things go wrong

Panic takes every output down with the sound, whichever surface raised it. An output whose window crashes reloads in place and puts back every picture it had, where each would have been by then — the record is kept by the app, not by the window. A picture whose file is missing says so in the log and on its cue.
