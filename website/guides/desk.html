Scenes and the deskPro

Who rides which fader, scene by scene, as a grid you can read from the back of the room — and a cue that moves the desk to match.

1. Tell Cuerunner about the desk

Cuerunner ▾ → Show Settings… → Channels. Pick how the desk is reached — OSC, MIDI, or MIDI over TCP — and the address or port it answers on. A MIDI-over-TCP desk is dialled at the port beside its address — 51325 is what an SQ and an Avantis both serve their MIDI on. The line under it reads back exactly what Cuerunner will send, so you can check it against the console's own documentation before anything is live.

An SQ is reached on a second port, and the two carry different facts. Its MIDI service — 51325, or a MIDI port — takes the channel mutes. Its own control service — TCP 51326 outbound, held open by Cuerunner with a keepalive datagram to the desk's UDP 51324 and a ping on the socket — takes the membership, and there is no other route that does: an SQ reached over a plain MIDI port can be muted and assigned nothing. None of that is a setting: 51326 is the desk's own port, and the cadence is Cuerunner's.

Then list the channels — the strips on the console, a name and a number each — and the faders the show drives, each with its own Count beside the heading: set it to the desk's size and the list is that long at once, so a show set up too large is one press from the right size. The two counts go different ways on the way down. A strip that goes leaves every scene and unpatches whoever was on it: a placement naming a strip that is gone is a cell nobody can read again, and a mic pointed at one opens nothing. A fader that goes takes every scene's row for that place with it and unpatches nobody — the mics stay where they are, and a fader grown back comes back empty. Which channel rides which fader is not set up here: that is the scene's decision, taken in the grid, so a chorus can ride one fader in one number and be split across the stage in the next. Each fader carries one desk number, for a desk that counts its DCAs differently from the show, and the row under it says how many scenes place anything on it. Under the faders is the one rule the grid cannot state for itself — Allow a channel to ride more than one fader: left on, a strip rides as many faders as a scene places it on; switched off, the grid keeps one fader per channel and a recall folds a leftover down to the lowest-numbered one. The line under the desk connection reads the desk's own ceiling back too, so a show larger than the desk model states is named there, with the number, and sent as it is.

Two fields this tab used to carry are deleted rather than deprecated: a strip has no fader select of its own, and a fader has one desk number in place of the note and address overrides that used to sit beside it. A show saved with either shape is refused when it is opened, naming what the build does not know, rather than converted — the strips, the faders and the placements are set up again here and in the grid.

2. Enter the cast

Cast is the people: a name, a part, the channel they are on and any note you need. Ensembles are groups you will open together — principals, chorus, band — so a scene can apply a whole section in one press instead of a row of them.

3. Build the scenes

Switch the window to Scenes, press Edit, and add one. The show’s faders run down the side and the scenes across the top, and each cell says which strips that scene puts on that fader, by the name the strip was given or the name of the performer standing on it. Every row ends in a blank box: press it, type a name into the filter, and pick the strip. Take a strip off with the ✕ beside its name. A strip can ride several faders — a chorus master on one and its members spread across others — unless Show Settings has that turned off (Channels → Allow a channel to ride more than one fader), where placing a strip on a row takes it off that scene's other rows.

An ensemble is the other half of building a row: the same box lists the ensembles the row does not apply, and picking one puts every member’s strip on the row, with a member you want left off shown beside it to switch off. Ensemble…, the last row of the box, is where a new one is built: name it, move the strips it should hold from Available to In the ensemble with the two arrows, and press Create and apply — the group joins the cast and this row rides it, as one edit you can undo. It is left there to apply to other rows and scenes. A strip nobody is cast on cannot be in a group, so it cannot be moved across: it stays a placement of the row's own, and the row carries exactly what it carried. Done when you have finished.

A scene answers for every channel, not only for the ones it places: a channel it leaves off every fader in that scene is closed by the recall, and a member excluded from an ensemble rides nothing and is closed with it. The leftmost column, All closed, is a scene like any other that way — it places nothing, so it closes every mic the show drives — and it is the one column that is never edited. Columns are a fixed width, so a show of two scenes reads the same as a show of twenty: what does not fit across the window scrolls inside the grid.

Out of edit mode the grid is for running: press Recall on a scene and the desk moves. The column the desk is currently on is marked, so the grid always says where you are, and Recall now appears above it when the desk has not been told everything the current scene asks for. Resync beside it re-sends that scene’s whole set: it is the press for when somebody has moved a fader or muted a mic by hand at the desk, which nothing here can see.

4. Put a scene in the cue list

Add a Scene cue (toolbar → More) and point it at a scene. GO now moves the desk as part of the show.

A recall sends two facts and no others: the membership — the channels the scene places on each fader, and the faders it takes them off — and then each mic’s mute. The order matters twice over. The membership comes first because it is what a mute is a state of: a channel the scene has just placed and opened is opened on the fader it was put on. And a mic whose fader changes keeps playing — it takes the new fader’s position and is never muted on the way there. Neither fact names a channel outside the show’s own set: the strips Show Settings → Channels lists are the boundary, and a mute carries the channel’s own number and never a fader’s, because a fader is a control group and closing one is not how a mic is silenced.

What goes out is only what changes, diffed against what Cuerunner last sent, and each write is remembered as it lands. Mics an earlier recall already opened are left alone, so a recall never bumps a live channel and a scene cue is safe to fire twice. Nothing here can see a fader somebody moved by hand at the desk — that is what Resync is for.

The membership goes over whatever route the show’s desk takes it on: an SQ reached over MIDI over TCP has it written on its own control service, TCP 51326, and an Avantis takes it as NRPN 40 on the desk link. A desk with no such route — a note desk, an OSC desk, or an SQ reached over a plain MIDI port — reports the gap and writes the mutes anyway rather than going quiet.

A scene cue with no scene chosen does nothing rather than closing every mic on the desk. That is deliberate: the dangerous reading of an empty scene is "close everything", and it is never what anyone meant.

5. Run it from the desk’s own keys

A console only reports a key press when the key is assigned to something the console can name, so Cuerunner reads GO and BACK from two mute groups — or from two MIDI notes beside them on the same Show Settings → Channels tab. Assign the groups to two spare keys at the desk and pressing them walks the show’s scenes — GO to the next, BACK to the one before, in the order the Scenes view shows them.

A group’s key toggles the group, so the desk’s indicator stays lit — which is what you want when the light itself is the cue that the show has moved. Where a press only ever means “next cue”, set a soft key to MIDI Note instead (Setup → Surface → Soft Controls on an SQ) and enter the number it transmits as the GO note. That key sends the note only while it is held, so the press steps the show once, the release does nothing, and nothing is left lit at the desk. An SQ’s soft keys transmit from 48 upward, one per key.

The note fields are offered on an SQ, whose own desk traffic Cuerunner cannot mistake for a hand on a key. On an Avantis the mute groups are notes and its surface keys latch, so the group route is the one to use there. Both routes arrive on the desk link — MIDI over TCP on 51325, or a MIDI port — which is the same connection a recall sends the mutes down; the SQ’s own control service on 51326 carries the membership and nothing a key press produces.
